  • The principle of least action: Every path between two points gets one number, the action S = ∫L dt with L = T − V. Vary the path, q → q + εη with η zero at both ends, and ask that S does not change to first order, δS = 0. Integrate by parts: the boundary term vanishes and the Euler–Lagrange equation is left, d/dt ∂L/∂q̇ − ∂L/∂q = 0, which for L = ½mq̇² − V is F = ma. With p = ∂L/∂q̇ and H = Σ pq̇ − L, write S = ∫(Σ pq̇ − H) dt and vary q and p independently: integrating the p δq̇ term by parts gives Hamilton’s equations, q̇ = ∂H/∂p and ṗ = −∂H/∂q. Why stationary? Feynman: every path counts, with a phase e^{iS/ħ}; near δS = 0 the phases agree and add up, elsewhere they cancel. The camera that brought you in rode such a path: a bead on a wire along its route, through an invented landscape. After a lecture by Erik Verlinde (University of Amsterdam). Sources: Goldstein, Poole & Safko, Classical Mechanics, 3rd ed., ch. 2 and §8.5; Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. II, ch. 19; Thornton & Marion, Classical Dynamics, 5th ed., ch. 7; Cline, Variational Principles in Classical Mechanics, ch. 9. Verlinde: F = ma from entropy
